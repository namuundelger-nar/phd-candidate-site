The Resource Potential of the Arctic: Problems and Prospects for Cooperation Between Russia and Other States

Research work report (3rd year) — Russian Presidential Academy of National Economy and Public Administration (RANEPA), Institute of Management and Regional Development, Faculty of Marketing and International Cooperation, Moscow, 2024.
Author: Namuundelger Narmandakh. Academic supervisor: E. A. Zaytseva.

Introduction

Interest in the Russian Arctic as a macro-region of the future has grown alongside a new stage of state policy aimed at its redevelopment through 2035. Around the Northern Sea Route (NSR), the strategy pursues five core tasks: building land transport infrastructure, forming a cargo base, developing the icebreaker/transport fleet, ensuring navigation safety, and centralizing management. The Arctic today generates roughly a tenth of Russia's GDP and 22% of its national exports.

This report focuses in particular on relations between Russia and Norway in the Arctic space, drawing on English-language foreign press coverage (US and Norwegian outlets) from the period of the COVID-19 pandemic and the war in Ukraine.

Object of study: the resource potential of the Arctic and Russia's cooperation on Arctic issues.

Subject of study: foreign-policy factors and events shaping Russia's cooperation with other states over Arctic resources and logistics routes, 2020–2023.

Hypothesis: (1) the Arctic zone is currently one of Russia's most promising regions; (2) Arctic resources — ports, logistics routes, oil and coal — are among Russia's key assets; (3) implementing the 2035 Arctic and Northern Sea Route strategy is a necessity.

Aim: to examine the resource potential, problems, and prospects of Russia's Arctic cooperation with other states.

Methodology: comparative analysis, generalization, and synthesis (theoretical); content analysis, statistics, classification, and historical analysis (empirical).

Chapter 1. Theoretical Aspects of Arctic Resource Potential

1.1 Oil Extraction Potential

Arctic oil reserves are estimated in the tens of billions of barrels and remain under-explored, representing significant interest for oil companies despite technical (low temperatures, unique geology), environmental (marine fauna risk, spill risk), and economic (high development costs, volatile world prices) challenges. Oil, gas, mining, and chemical companies are the leading employers in the Russian Far North; by the end of 2023 more than 110,000 new jobs were expected to be created in the Arctic zone. At the same time, industrial enterprises are responsible for roughly 70% of the region's most severe environmental problems, and Russia has designated several "disaster zones" where pollution exceeds permissible levels.

1.2 The Northern Sea Route as a Logistics Corridor

Maritime shipping is the primary means of supplying Arctic settlements. More than 30 million tons of cargo move along the NSR annually. Defined in Russian law as the country's "historically established national unified transport communication in the Arctic," the NSR is closely tied to Russia's "pivot to the East" strategy and draws mixed reactions abroad: Canada and Denmark view it as a rival to the Northwest Passage, while most other states have not taken an explicit public stance.

1.3 The Role of Russian Ports and Settlements

About 2.5 million people live in the Russian Arctic, over 82,000 of them Indigenous. Roughly a quarter practice reindeer herding, fishing, or hunting and lead a nomadic lifestyle. Population outflow from the region (e.g., roughly 3,000 people left Murmansk Oblast in a few months of 2022) is driven by harsh climate and weak infrastructure; 14% of the Arctic workforce is employed on a rotational ("shift") basis. Ports underpin economic development, defense capability, and the preservation of Indigenous ways of life.

Chapter 2. Russia's Cooperation with Other States on Arctic Issues

2.1 Russian Settlements in Svalbard, Norway

Under the 1920 Svalbard Treaty, Norway holds sovereignty over the archipelago while treaty parties (more than 50 states today) retain equal rights to exploit its natural resources; Norway is barred from establishing naval bases or using the territory for military purposes. The USSR was recognized in 1947 as having special economic interests there alongside Norway. The main Russian settlement, Barentsburg, uses both Norwegian and Russian as official languages and requires no visa for Russian citizens; despite import restrictions on Russian goods following sanctions, local residents — including those of Norwegian origin — have remained well-disposed toward Russia since the start of its military operation in Ukraine.

2.2 Russia's Role in the Arctic Council

The Arctic Council brings together eight Arctic states — Russia, the US, Canada, Denmark (with Greenland and the Faroe Islands), Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Iceland — plus Indigenous permanent participants. Russia, Norway, and the US co-chaired work on the legally binding 2013 search-and-rescue cooperation agreement. Russia positions itself as an active participant supporting sustainable development, environmental protection, and adherence to international law in dispute resolution.

2.3 New Russia–China Cooperation on the Northern Sea Route

Russia and China are deepening strategic partnership around NSR development, including port infrastructure, logistics, and trade volume growth — a trend that concerns the Arctic Council and NATO, given the NSR's potential to strengthen Russian maritime logistics.

Chapter 3. Development Strategy and Solutions for Russia's Arctic Zone

3.1 New Cooperation as a Solution to Arctic Zone Problems

Russia–Norway cooperation shows the greatest promise in tourism, cultural heritage, oil extraction, fishing, and logistics. In 2021–2023 Russia chaired the Arctic Council, a flexible forum for addressing climate change and sustainable development, even as tensions persist over disputed issues such as Svalbard's status.

3.2 Arctic Zone Development Strategy Through 2035

The strategy calls for modernizing 14 ports and terminals from Murmansk to Vladivostok, increasing NSR cargo volume to 80 million tons/year by 2024 (per presidential decree), and building 153 new icebreaker and transport vessels, alongside expanded satellite reconnaissance and hydrographic/hydrometeorological support. Rosatom has been given unified authority over NSR shipping management. Roughly 1.8 trillion rubles is earmarked for NSR development through 2035, targeting a carrying capacity of 200 million tons by 2030.

3.3 Russia's Image in the Arctic Region

Russia's Arctic Council participation, leading role in resource extraction, and geopolitical weight together shape an image combining historical roots, economic activity, international cooperation, environmental stewardship, and strategic significance.

Conclusion

The Arctic's resource potential — oil, gas, minerals, fish, tourism, and transport infrastructure — offers substantial opportunities for economic development, but its exploitation faces environmental risk, harsh climatic conditions, infrastructure difficulty, and security challenges tied to the vulnerability of local communities. International cooperation, including Russia's engagement with the US, Canada, Norway, and Denmark on shared research, safety standards, and resource-use rules, is essential to sustainable and responsible development of the region and to reducing potential interstate conflict.
