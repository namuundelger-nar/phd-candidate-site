"One Belt, One Road": Evolution, Challenges and Prospects for International Cooperation

Bachelor's Thesis — Russian Presidential Academy of National Economy and Public Administration (RANEPA), Faculty of Economics, Moscow, 2025.
Author: Narmandakh Namuundelger. Academic supervisor: Prof. Alexander Ivanovich Sukharev, Dr. Sc.
English translation of the Russian original.

Introduction

Energy resources are becoming scarce, consumption is growing at unsustainable rates, global financial and economic crises are becoming more frequent and prolonged, and overproduction is creating a surplus of goods, which has led to falling prices for major raw materials on the world market. At a time when the global political climate has cooled owing to disagreements among the leading world powers over the settlement of the crises in Ukraine and Syria, the Chinese leadership has launched the global "One Belt, One Road" concept and, as a global power, is building the "Silk Road" economic zone across Eurasia. Researchers believe that this joint initiative with other countries will make an important contribution to strengthening cooperation between countries around the world. As a result, 65 countries around China have expressed interest in participating in the "Silk Road" project, which is to be implemented within the framework of the "One Belt, One Road" concept.

Since the launch of the initiative, China's political, social, economic and cultural influence in the region has increased. China continues to invest heavily in the development of international transport and logistics infrastructure, relocating domestic production abroad and investing in the energy and mining sectors. Many researchers have described these investments as "fictitious." Another group of researchers emphasises that the implementation of "One Belt, One Road" depends on cooperation between countries, or that China lacks a developed policy framework. For this reason, the present study was undertaken to analyse the problems facing China's "One Belt, One Road" initiative.

Object of the study: the "One Belt, One Road" initiative.

Subject of the study: the economic, political and social aspects of the initiative that affect international cooperation.

Aim of the study: to analyse the evolution of the "One Belt, One Road" initiative, to assess the challenges and prospects for international cooperation, and to identify the factors that contribute to the effective development of the initiative.

Objectives:

— to analyse and assess the external and internal challenges facing China's "One Belt, One Road" initiative;
— to examine the essence of China's "One Belt, One Road" initiative and the current status of its implementation;
— to identify the needs and requirements for implementing Mongolia's "Steppe Road" project within the construction of the China–Mongolia–Russia economic corridor.

Methodological basis: a comprehensive approach combining theoretical analysis with empirical data, drawing on concepts of international cooperation, economic integration and geopolitics; analytical and comparative methods to assess the dynamics of the initiative and its impact on international relations.

Research methods: inductive and deductive document analysis, primary and secondary data analysis, analysis and synthesis, generalisation, SWOT analysis, and PEST analysis.

Research hypothesis: "The One Belt, One Road initiative will strengthen international cooperation and economic integration in the region, despite existing geopolitical challenges, provided that participating countries ensure effective governance and coordination."

Structure: the thesis consists of six parts — an introduction, three main chapters (theoretical foundations, current state of implementation, and an analytical section on problems and prospects), a conclusion and a list of references.

Chapter 1. Theoretical Foundations of the "One Belt, One Road" Project

1.1 Evolution of the "One Belt, One Road" Concept

The term "Silk Road" was first used by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen (1833–1905) in 1877. According to the Encyclopaedia Britannica, from about 100 BC camel caravans travelled a route some 6,400 km long, starting at Chang'an, passing through China along the Great Wall, heading north-west to the Pamir Mountains, crossing Afghanistan and reaching the eastern shore of the Mediterranean. The route carried silk to the West and wool, gold and silver to the East. It grew increasingly dangerous after the fall of Rome, was reopened in the Mongol period, and Marco Polo travelled along it in the 13th century. Its importance grew during the rise of the Mongols, when it became a major artery linking West and East.

The restoration of this historically important road network is being carried out on China's initiative. From 1978 to 2010, average annual growth of Chinese government investment in road and transport infrastructure was 17.3 percent; under the "Go West" programme (1999–2009), investment in the western region's transport infrastructure increased sharply, exceeding the national average by 9.1 percent by 2010. In September 2013, during President Xi Jinping's first visit to Central Asia, the "Silk Road" cooperation model was presented as a "Silk Road Economic Belt" across Eurasia, in a speech at Nazarbayev University, Kazakhstan. China divided the future "New Silk Road" into five sub-regions: Central Asia (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan); the oil- and gas-producing Middle Eastern states; the Caucasus and Eastern Europe; the Russian Federation; and Afghanistan, Pakistan and India.

On 3 October 2013, in a speech to the Indonesian parliament, Xi Jinping proposed the complementary "21st Century Maritime Silk Road," aimed at strengthening maritime cooperation with ASEAN. These two concepts — the Silk Road Economic Belt and the 21st Century Maritime Silk Road — were combined and are today formulated as "One Belt, One Road." China presents the initiative as strengthening connectivity and development for all participating countries rather than as an instrument of Chinese power, proposing a "community of common destiny for mankind" founded on mutual respect, equality and mutually beneficial cooperation. The project covers an area inhabited by 65 countries and 4.4 billion people, producing 63 percent of world GDP.

1.2 The Essence of the "One Belt, One Road" Initiative

China's "One Belt, One Road" initiative is a development strategy comprising both the Silk Road Economic Belt and the 21st Century Maritime Silk Road, aimed at expanding economic cooperation and facilitating resource flows among participating countries. China's foreign trade turnover reached USD 3.4 trillion in 2012, making it the world's largest exporter and second-largest importer. On 28 March 2015, China's National Development and Reform Commission published a strategic document setting out the goals and vision for jointly building the two Silk Roads. On 1 October 2016 the IMF granted the Chinese yuan reserve currency status. The initiative is a key factor in securing China's long-term energy supply and responding to the global middle class, projected to reach 4.9 billion people by 2030.

1.3 Main Areas of Cooperation

The initiative envisages six land corridors connecting Asia, Europe and Africa along five routes: connecting China with Europe through Central Asia and Russia; connecting China with the Middle East through Central Asia; connecting China with Southeast Asia, South Asia and the Indian Ocean; connecting China with Europe through the East China Sea and inland waters; and connecting China with the South Pacific through the South China Sea. These form six international economic cooperation corridors: (1) the New Eurasian Land Bridge; (2) China–Mongolia–Russia; (3) China–Central Asia–West Asia; (4) China–Indochina Peninsula; (5) China–Pakistan; and (6) Bangladesh–China–India–Myanmar.

The New Eurasian Land Bridge runs from Alashankou in Xinjiang to Rotterdam via Kazakhstan, Russia, Belarus and Poland, linking cities such as Chongqing–Duisburg, Wuhan–Mělník/Pardubice, Chengdu–Łódź, and Zhengzhou–Hamburg. The China–Mongolia–Russia Economic Corridor emerged from a trilateral summit in Dushanbe (September 2014) linking China's Silk Road Economic Belt, Russia's modernised Eurasian land bridge (Trans-Siberian Railway), and Mongolia's Steppe Road; a medium-term roadmap was approved at a second summit in Ufa (July 2015). The China–Central Asia–West Asia corridor covers five Central Asian states plus Iran and Turkey; the China–Indochina Peninsula corridor covers highway construction and new financing models with five Indochinese states; the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor links Kashgar with the port of Gwadar; and the Bangladesh–China–India–Myanmar corridor was discussed from 2013 onward. The initiative's five goals are policy coordination, infrastructure connectivity, unimpeded trade, financial integration, and people-to-people bonds, financed in part through the USD 40 billion Silk Road Fund and the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (57 founding members as of December 2015).

Chapter 2. Implementation of the "One Belt, One Road" Project

2.1 Implementation in Central Asia

China is the largest trading partner of Kazakhstan and Turkmenistan, and the second-largest of Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan (trade with Central Asia reached USD 45 billion in 2014). Kazakhstan's "Nurly Zhol" programme (USD 9 billion over three years) links to 52 joint projects worth USD 24 billion. Tajikistan built a 48.6 km railway (2016, USD 72 million); Uzbekistan built the 19.2 km Kamchik tunnel (2016, USD 1.6 billion); Kyrgyzstan financed road construction partly through a resources-for-infrastructure deal with Zijin Mining Group (USD 660 million for a 60% stake in a gold mine). A long-planned China–Kyrgyzstan–Uzbekistan trilateral railway (476 km) has seen its cost estimate rise from USD 900 million to USD 6.5 billion without resolution.

2.2 Cooperation and Interaction of the PRC with Participating States

The Eurasian Economic Union (EAEU, in force since 1 January 2015; members Russia, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Armenia, Kyrgyzstan) represents a parallel integration framework whose projects intersect indirectly with the Belt and Road through Russia's participation, including a planned Moscow–Kazan high-speed railway partly financed by China. The China–Mongolia–Russia corridor's western branch is shaped by geography (Mongolia's location between Russia and China, and the historic "Tea Road"), by the shared goal of limiting third-country influence, and by relatively low required additional capital investment since transport networks already partly exist. Chinese researchers note that a route through Mongolia offers the shortest path from Northeast Asia to Europe and low-cost access to Mongolian metal and energy deposits.

2.3 Russia and the PRC in the Implementation of the Project

Russia's interest lies in increased Trans-Siberian Railway traffic and transit fees, given its co-ownership of the Ulaanbaatar Railway. China provided a USD 25 billion loan for a gas pipeline in 2006, and cooperation deepened following Western sanctions on Russia over Crimea. Chinese investment in the Russian economy reached USD 1 billion by 2014. China's trade with Central Asia grew from USD 1 billion (2000) to USD 30 billion (2012), backed by major energy agreements with Kazakhstan (USD 30 billion, 2013) and Turkmenistan (USD 7.31 billion, 2006), alongside infrastructure commitments including the Chongqing–Xinjiang–Europe railway and the Kashgar–Irkeshtam highway.

Chapter 3. Problems and Prospects of the "One Belt, One Road" Project

3.1 Problems and Challenges

The initiative aims to create regional value chains, relocate economically inefficient and environmentally harmful industries abroad, support China's north-western provinces, and increase sales for Chinese construction companies — alongside exporting Chinese standards, technologies and brands. It forms an important part of China's 13th Five-Year Plan and offers CIS states a path to join the Eurasian transport and logistics network.

3.2 Implementation of the Chinese "Silk Road" Project

The initiative functions as an exercise in Chinese soft power as much as infrastructure-building. Of the 68 countries China cooperates with, 23 have low credit ratings, and eight (Djibouti, Kyrgyzstan, Laos, the Maldives, Mongolia, Pakistan, Tajikistan, Montenegro) carry the highest credit risk; borrowing is projected to push public debt in Djibouti, Mongolia and Montenegro above 80 percent of GDP by 2030, and in the Maldives to 109 percent. Loan conditions often require recipients to purchase at least half of related goods and services from Chinese companies. A joint Nazarbayev University/J-Invest-Consulting survey of 300 Kazakh and Kyrgyz representatives found 96 percent viewed Chinese investment as beneficial, yet 80 percent opposed Chinese immigration and majorities expressed concern about environmental impact and national security. Kazakhstan's 2016 land-ownership protests illustrated persistent anti-Chinese sentiment rooted partly in the Sino-Soviet conflict of the 1960s–70s.

3.3 Development Prospects of the Megaproject

China's interest in Central Asia combines securing uninterrupted energy supply, easing regional anxiety about a "Chinese threat," and pursuing counter-terrorism goals after the withdrawal of US and allied forces from Afghanistan. Soft-power tools include 30,000 government scholarships and cultural/educational programmes, alongside a language-policy principle of "develop Chinese, maintain Russian, learn English." On 22 August 2014, Xi Jinping presented the Belt and Road vision to the Mongolian parliament, endorsing Mongolia's parallel "Steppe Road" concept (five links: roads, railways, oil and gas pipelines, and power lines). Russian President Putin voiced support for coordinating the Steppe Road with Russian projects during a September 2014 visit to Mongolia. At a trilateral summit in Dushanbe, Mongolian President Ts. Elbegdorj proposed a regular three-year summit cycle in Ulaanbaatar; a further summit in Ufa (2015) approved a medium-term cooperation roadmap. Mongolia's central region — through which the Steppe Road passes — holds 78 percent of national GDP, 89 percent of infrastructure and 62.2 percent of the population, making it highly attractive for investment despite chronic financing gaps (illustrated by decades of delay on the China–Kyrgyzstan–Uzbekistan railway and Mongolia's own new north–south lines, feasibility-studied by McKinsey and partly built by Samsung C&T).

A jointly built economic corridor is projected to let Mongolia serve as a trade and infrastructure bridge between Russia and China, attract large-scale investment and new jobs, strengthen Northeast Asian economic integration, bring mineral deposits into productive use, and let Mongolian companies participate in major international projects — provided the corridor preserves a balance of interests between Mongolia's only two neighbours.

Conclusion

As the world economy becomes increasingly globalised, the gap in national economic development is widening. China's Belt and Road policy supplies its economy with needed raw materials, metals and energy while generating returns on overseas investment; the initiative has created some 240,000 new jobs and generated USD 2 billion in tax revenue across participating countries via projects such as the China–Europe Railway Express and the New International Land–Sea Trade Corridor. At the same time, the initiative raises real economic risks — the infrastructure being built is still largely experimental — and China's international image as the initiating country needs improvement. Concerns persist that Mongolia could face combined pressure from both neighbours, though it is doubtful that China and Russia's core interests will always align on major issues; the trilateral programme can instead be read as both neighbours accepting Mongolia's own foreign-policy approach.

The initiative faces resistance from the United States, Japan and India — who have criticised it as "debt-trap diplomacy," with India pursuing its own Indo-Pacific and "Asia–Africa Growth Corridor" initiatives with US and Japanese backing. Kazakh researchers likewise note public unease over the scale of Chinese business presence. As the ability of countries to develop in isolation becomes increasingly limited, the principle of joint implementation and shared development that underlies "One Belt, One Road" has rightly attracted broad international interest — though its long-term success will depend on whether participating countries safeguard their own national interests, and whether China succeeds in pairing its economic ambitions with a genuinely equal partnership model.
